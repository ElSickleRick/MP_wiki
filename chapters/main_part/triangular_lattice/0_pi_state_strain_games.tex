\subsubsection{Uniform Hopping}

I investigate the effect of uniformly varying hopping amplitudes with one edge case reproducing a lattice topolgically equivalent to the square lattice. . I modify the hopping amplitudes in the unit cell of the two-site unit cell as follows 

\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{uniform_hopping_lattice.jpeg}
\end{center}

This gives the real space Hamiltonian 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		H = t \sum_{i\sigma}\bigg(&-b_{i\sigma}^\dagger b_{i+t_1;\sigma} - b_{i\sigma}^\dagger b_{i-t_1;\sigma} + a_{i\sigma}^\dagger a_{i+t_1;\sigma} + a_{i\sigma}^\dagger a_{i-t_1;\sigma} \\
		&- b_{i\sigma}^\dagger a_{i\sigma} + b_{i\sigma}^\dagger a_{i+t_2;\sigma} + (1-2\eta)b_{i\sigma}^\dagger a_{i-t_1+t_2;\sigma} + (1-2\gamma) b_{i\sigma}^\dagger a_{i+t_1}  \\
		&-a_{i\sigma}^\dagger b_{i\sigma} + a_{i\sigma}^\dagger b_{i-t_2;\sigma} +(1-2\eta)a_{i\sigma}^\dagger b_{i-t_1;\sigma} + (1-2\gamma) a_{i\sigma}^\dagger b_{i+t_1-t_2;\sigma}\bigg).
	\end{split}
\end{align}

Going to reciprocal space:

\begin{equation}
	H = t \sum_{k\sigma} \Psi_{k\sigma}^\dagger \left(\vec{h}\cdot\vec{\sigma}\right)\Psi_{k\sigma},
\end{equation}

with:

\begin{equation}
	\vec{h}(\vec{q}) = 2t 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\cos(q_3) - (\eta + \gamma)\cos(q_3) \\
		\sin(q_2) + (\eta-\gamma)\sin(q_3) \\
		\cos(q_1)
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\textcolor{blue}{Sanity check: for $\eta=\gamma=0$ one recovers the ordinary $\pi$-flux Hmailtonian}. \\

The (possible) Dirac points are at $\vec{h} = 0$. The $\sigma_x$ suggests that there are two sets of solutions determined by:

\begin{equation}
	\cos(q_3) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \eta + \gamma = 1.
\end{equation}

For the first case use $\cos(q_1) = \cos(q_2+q_3) = \cos(q_2)\cos(q_3) - \sin(q_2)\sin(q_3)$

\begin{gather*}
	\begin{align}
		(\sigma_z) &\Rightarrow -\sin(q_2)\sin(q_3) = 0 \\
		&\Leftrightarrow \sin(q_2) = 0
	\end{align} \\
	\begin{align}
		(\sigma_y) &\Rightarrow (\eta - \gamma)\sin(q_3) = 0 \\
		&\Leftrightarrow \eta = \gamma 	
	\end{align}
\end{gather*}

For the second case use $q_1 = \pm \pi/2$, $(\sigma_y)$ then gives the solution for $q_y$ (using trigonometric identities):

\begin{align*}
	q_x = \frac{\pi}{2} &\Rightarrow q_y = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \arctan(1-\frac{1}{\gamma}) \\
	q_x = -\frac{\pi}{2} &\Rightarrow q_y = -\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \arctan(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})
\end{align*}

I reclassify the Dirac points by the condition on $\eta$ and $\gamma$: \\

\underline{$\eta = \gamma:$} \\

\begin{equation}
	\vec{D} = \frac{\pi}{2}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		1 \\
		1/\sqrt{3}
	\end{pmatrix}, \quad
	\vec{D}^\prime = -\frac{\pi}{2}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		1 \\
		1/\sqrt{3}
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\underline{$\eta + \gamma = 1:$} \\

\begin{equation}
	\vec{D} = 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\frac{\pi}{2} \\
		\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\arctan(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})
	\end{pmatrix}, \quad
	\vec{D}^\prime = -
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\frac{\pi}{2} \\
		\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\arctan(1-\frac{1}{\gamma})
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

In both cases there are two Dirac points The two cases meet at $\eta = \gamma = \frac12$, wich will be discussed in detail in the next paragraph. 

\paragraph{$\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\eta} = \boldsymbol{\gamma}}$: Towards the square lattice} \hfill \\ 

I focus on the case $\eta = \gamma$. For $\gamma = \frac12$ one recovers a lattice of $\pi$-flux-rhombi. The idea being that this is topologcally equivalent to a $\pi$-flux-square lattice. $\gamma = 0$ just gives back the $\pi$-flux triangular lattice. It is known that in both edge cases  there is no pseudomagnetic field (also these notes as well as \cite{Sun2023}). \\


The Hamiltonian reads 

\begin{equation}
	\Rightarrow\vec{h}(\vec{q}) = 2t 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		(1-2\gamma)\cos(q_3) \\
		\sin(q_2) \\
		\cos(q_1)
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

I linearize the letter around $\vec{D}$

\begin{equation}
	\vec{h}(\vec{D}+\vec{p}) \approx \underbrace{\vec{h}(\vec{D})}_{\equiv \vec{h}^0_{\vec{D}}} + \underbrace{\left(\nabla_{\vec{q}} \vec{h}(\vec{q})\right) \cdot \vec{p}}_{\equiv \vec{h}_{\vec{D}}^1}
\end{equation}

By definition $\vec{h}_{\vec{D}}^0 = 0$. However, before making use of this fact, one still has to carry out the expansion in terms of strain. Furtheremore

\begin{equation}
	\vec{h}_{\vec{D}}^1 \equiv  = 2t
	\begin{pmatrix}
		-(1-2\gamma)\sin(K_3)p_3 \\
		\cos(K_2)p_2 \\
		-\sin(K_1)p_1
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

A pseudomegnetic field would be generated by expanding $\vec{h}_0$ in strain. Expanding $\vec{h}_1$, on the other hand, only multiplies $\vec{p}$ and therefore cant give a pseudomagnetic field, at least to lowest order. \\

Here comes the catch; since the relevant term in the expansion of $t(\vec{\delta}_{ij})e^{i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}}$, namely

\begin{equation}
	-t\beta e^{i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}}\left( \vec{\delta}_{ij} \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij} \right),
\end{equation}

are insensible to sign of the hopping direction, this reproduces the entries of $\vec{h}_{\vec{D}}$ with a factor. This then vanishes by definition. One can escape this paradigm by mixing non-parallel hopping directions in the same component of $\vec{h}(\vec{q})$, as in the next example. \\

\textcolor{red}{This statement only uses the form of $\vec{h}$. Shouldnt it be possible to formulate a mathematicel criterion that only depends on $\vec{h}$ that distinguishes wether the system is capable of producing pseudo-magnetic fields?}


\paragraph{$\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\eta} + \boldsymbol{\gamma} = 1}$} \hfill \\

This choice of parameters leads to:

\begin{equation}
	t(1-2\eta) \overset{\eta = 1-\gamma}{\longrightarrow} -t(1-2\gamma).
\end{equation}

meaning that the hoppin amplitudes along $\pm\vec{\delta}_3$, however the hopping phases alternate between $0$ and $\pi$. This of course alters the flux pattern of the lattice. Acknowledging this fact it is clear that this lattice can not be transformed into the $\pi$-flux triangular lattice by choice of paramter. (This is actaully trivial, considering that $\eta = \gamma = 0$ does not satisfy $\eta + \gamma = 1$.) 

\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{uniform_hopping_flux_pattern.jpeg}
\end{center}

The picture shows the flux pattern in the case that $\gamma < \frac12$. \\

The Hamiltonian reads 

\begin{equation}
	\vec{h}(\vec{q}) = 2t 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		0 \\
		\sin(q_3) + (1-2\gamma)\sin(q_2) \\
		\cos(q_1)
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

Coming from the discussion in the last paragraph I expect that this Hamitlonian should be able to display pseudo-magnetic fields, since the (non-parallel!) hopping-directions $\vec{q}_3$ and $\vec{q}_2$ mix in the second component. Furthermore, since in the third component only $q_1 = q_x$ appears, I expect $A_x = 0$. \\

Helpful identities: 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\sin(D_2) = \cos(D_3) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{2\gamma-1}{\sqrt{\gamma^2 + (1-\gamma)^2}}, \\
		\sin(D_3) = \cos(D_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^2 + (1-\gamma)^2}}
	\end{split}
\end{align}

and 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\left(\vec{\delta}_2 \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_2\right) &= \frac14 \left(u_{xx} + \sqrt{3}uxy + \sqrt{3} u_{yx} + 3u_{yy}\right) \\
		\left(\vec{\delta}_3 \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_3\right) &= \frac14 \left(u_{xx} - \sqrt{3}uxy - \sqrt{3} u_{yx} + 3u_{yy}\right) 	
	\end{split}
\end{align}


I begin by linearizing the Hamiltonian: 

\begin{align}
	h_{\vec{D}}^0 &= 2t
	\begin{pmatrix}
		0 \\
		\sin(D_3) + (1-2\gamma)\sin(D_2) \\
		\cos(D_1) 
	\end{pmatrix}  = 0\\
	\vec{h}_{\vec{D}}^1 &= 2t 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		0 \\
		\cos(D_3)p_3 + (1-2\gamma)\cos(D_2)p_2 \\
		-\sin(D_1)p_1
	\end{pmatrix} = 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		0 \\
		f(\gamma) p_y\\
		-p_x
	\end{pmatrix},
\end{align}	

with 

\begin{equation}
	f(\gamma) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{2\gamma-1}{\sqrt{\gamma^2 + (1-\gamma)^2}}.
\end{equation}

The next step is to reinstate the hopping directions. Starting with the $\sigma_z$ components I have 

\begin{equation}
	t(\vec{\delta}_1)e^{i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_1} + t(-\vec{\delta}_1)e^{-i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_1}.
\end{equation}

Expanding in strain (and only keeping the relevant term) I get

\begin{equation}
	-t\beta \cos(D_1) \left( \vec{\delta}_1 \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_1\right) = 0,
\end{equation}

as was expected. Doing the same for the $\sigma_y$-component I get

\begin{equation}
	-i\left[t(\vec{\delta}_3)e^{i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_3} - t(-\vec{\delta}_3)e^{-i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_3}\right] + -i(1-2\gamma)\left[ t(\vec{\delta}_2)e^{i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_2} - t(-\vec{\delta}_2)e^{-i\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\delta}_2} \right]
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
	-2t\beta\left(\sin(D_3)\left(\vec{\delta}_3 \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_3\right)+ (1-2\gamma)\sin(D_2)\left( \vec{\delta}_2 \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_2\right)\right)
\end{equation}


This term can be evaluated to

\begin{equation}
	-f(\gamma)\frac{t\beta}{2(2\gamma-1)}\left(\frac{1-(2\gamma-1)^2}{\sqrt{3}}(u_{xx} + 3u_{yy}) - (1+(2\gamma-1)^2)(u_{xy}+u_{yx})\right),
\end{equation}

which is by all means ugly but still a pseudo vectorpotential. \textcolor{red}{This calculation is rather involved. I should check it using a CAS. Thi should vanish at $\eta = \gamma = \frac12$ (or alternatively can be gauged away). Why is this not the case?}
