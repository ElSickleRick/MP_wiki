\paragraph{Bands} \hfill \\

The dispersion relation is shown in the following picture 

\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{dispersion_relation_pi_flux_state.png} 
\end{center}

This plot was generatey by numerically calculating the eigenvalues of $H$ on a $(q_x, q_y)$-grid. The lowest band (flat band) is doubly degenerate. \\

The characteristic polynomial of \ref{Hpirs} is (this was generate by mathematica)

\begin{equation}
	x^6-12x^4-8x^3+x^2(30 + 2\Delta_{\vec{q}})+x(24+8\Delta_{\vec{q}})+8\Delta_{\vec{q}}-8 
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{units!} with 

\begin{equation}
	\Delta_{\vec{q}} = -\cos(4q_2)+\cos(4q_3)-\cos(4q_1).
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{I should verify this uisng Cayley-Hamilton.} To derive analytical expressions for the bands I first divide out the two flat bands (i.e. polynomial division by $(x+2)^2$). The result is

\begin{equation}
	x^4-4x^3+8x-2+2\Delta_{\vec{q}}.
\end{equation}

To make progress I postulate (judging from the numeriacl plot of the dispersion) that the above polynomial can be written as a product of two quadratic polynomials. Furtheremore, the solutions of said qaudratic equations must be centered around $t$. The p-q forumla suggests that the linear coefficents of both quadratic polynomials must therefore be $-2t$.

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\Rightarrow x^4-4x^3+8x-2+2\Delta_{\vec{q}} &=(x^2-2x+a)\cdot(x^2-2x +b) \\
		&= x^4-4x^3+x^2(4+a+b)-2x(a+b)+ab.
	\end{split}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
	\Rightarrow a+b \overset{!}{=} -4, \quad ab \overset{!}{=} -2+2\Delta_{\vec{q}}.
\end{equation}

These equations are solved by 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		a_{1,2} &= -2 \pm \sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}} \\
		b_{1,2} &= -2 \mp \sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}.
	\end{split}
\end{align}

Both solution lead to the same result ($a$ and $b$ are exchanged in the second soltuion wrt the first one). This gives the following expressions for the bands 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\varepsilon_1 (\vec{q}) &= -2t \\
		\varepsilon_2 (\vec{q}) &= -2t \\
		\varepsilon_3 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 -\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t \\
		\varepsilon_4 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 -\sqrt{3-\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t \\
		\varepsilon_5 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 +\sqrt{3-\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t \\
		\varepsilon_6 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 +\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t.
	\end{split}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
	\Delta_{\vec{q}} = -\cos(4q_2)+\cos(4q_3)-\cos(4q_1).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Dirac Points} \hfill \\

\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{touching_points_4th_band.png}
\end{center}

Shown above is the 4th band (green band). The black rectangle is the First Brillouin zone. Also shown are the position of the various band-touching points. $F$ the Dirac points at 1/2- (and 5/6-) filling. At 2/3-filling the Dirac points are at $C$ and $E$. \\


Now I derive the coordiantes. It is clear from the expressions above that bands 3 and 4 as well as bands 5 and 6 touch iff 

\begin{gather}
	6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}} = 0 \notag \\
	\Leftrightarrow -\cos(4q_2)+\cos(4q_3)-\cos(4q_1) = 3 \notag \\
	\Leftrightarrow 2\sin(2q_x)\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) - \cos(4q_x) = 3.
\end{gather}

This can easily be solved considering the ranges of sine and cosine. A possible parametrization of the solution is 

\begin{align}
	q_x &= (-1)^s \frac{\pi}{4} + m_1 \pi \\
	q_y &= (-1)^s \frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{3}} +(m_1 + m_2)\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}},
\end{align}

where

\begin{equation*}
	s \in [0,1], \quad m_1, m_2 \in \mathbb{Z}.
\end{equation*}

Both $(s = 0, m_1 = 0, m_2 = 0)$ and $(s = 1, m_1 = 0, m_2 = 0)$ are in the first Brillouin zone:

\begin{equation}
	\vec{F} = \frac{\pi}{4}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		1 \\
		1/\sqrt{3}
	\end{pmatrix}, \quad 
	\vec{F}^\prime = -\frac{\pi}{4}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		1 \\
		1/\sqrt{3}
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}.

On the other hand, bands 4 and 5 touch when 

\begin{gather}
	\sqrt{3-\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}} = 0 \notag \\
	\Leftrightarrow 2\Delta_{\vec{q}} = -3 \\
	\Leftrightarrow 4\sin(2q_x)\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) - 2\cos(4q_x) = -3 \notag
\end{gather}

Using $\cos(2x) = 1-2\sin^2(x)$ I obtain

\begin{gather}
	\sin^2(2q_x) + \sin(2q_x)\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) + \frac14 = 0 \notag \\
	\Rightarrow \sin(2q_x) = \frac12\left( -\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) \pm \sqrt{\sin^2(2\sqrt{3}q_y) - 1}\right)
\end{gather}

The expression under the square root gives a condition for $q_y$. It tourns out there are two distinct sets of solutions (meaning there are two distinct sets of symmetry-equivalent touching points).

\begin{align}
	q_x &= (-1)^s \frac{\pi}{12} + m_1  \pi \\
	q_y &= (-1)^{s+1} \frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{3}} + (m_1+m_2)\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}
\end{align}

and 

\begin{align}
	q_x &= (-1)^s\frac{5\pi}{12} + m_1 \pi \\
	q_y &= (-1)^{s+1}\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{3}} + (m_1 + m_2)\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}},
\end{align}

with the usual parmeters. Once more, there are two of each in the first Brillouin zone corresponding to $(s = 0, m_1 = 0, m_2 = 0)$ and $(s = 1, m_1 = 0, m_2 = 0)$:

\begin{gather}
	\begin{split}
		\vec{C} = \frac{\pi}{12}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			1 \\
			-\sqrt{3}
		\end{pmatrix}, \quad 
		\vec{C}^\prime = \frac{\pi}{12}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			-1 \\
			\sqrt{3}
		\end{pmatrix},\\	
		\vec{E} = \frac{\pi}{12}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			5 \\
			-\sqrt{3}
		\end{pmatrix}, \quad
		\vec{E}^\prime = \frac{\pi}{12}
		\begin{pmatrix}
			-5 \\
			\sqrt{3}
		\end{pmatrix}.	
	\end{split}
\end{gather}

\paragraph{Linearization of $H$/ Field Theory} \hfill \\

To derive an effective (linear) theory in the vicinity of the touching points I linearize the Hamiltonian in $\vec{q}$.

\begin{equation}
	H(\vec{K}+ \vec{p}) \approx \underbrace{H(\vec{K})}_{\equiv H_{\vec{K}}^0} + \underbrace{\vec{p} \cdot \left(\vec{\nabla}_{\vec{q}}H(\vec{q})\right)\bigg\rvert_{\vec{q} = \vec{K}}}_{\equiv H_{\vec{K}}^1} + \mathcal{O}(p^2).
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	H_{\vec{K}}^0 =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		0 & e^{iK_2} & -e^{-iK_3} & 0 & e^{-iK_2} & e^{iK_3} \\
		e^{-iK_2} & 0 & 2i\sin(K_1) & e^{iK_2} & 0 & 0 \\
		-e^{iK_3} & -2i\sin(K_1) & 0 & e^{-iK_3} & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & e^{-iK_2} & e^{iK_3} & 0 & e^{iK_2} & e^{-iK_3}\\
		e^{iK_2} & 0 & 0 & e^{-iK_2} & 0 & 2\cos(K_1) \\
		e^{-iK_3} & 0 & 0 & e^{iK_3} & 2\cos(K_1) & 0
	\end{pmatrix} 
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	H_{\vec{K}}^1 =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		0 & ie^{iK_2}p_2 & ie^{-iK_3}p_3 & 0 & -ie^{-iK_2}p_2 & ie^{iK_3}p_3 \\
		-ie^{-iK_2}p_2 & 0 & 2i\cos(p_1) & ie^{iK_2}p_2 & 0 & 0 \\
		-ie^{iK_3}p_3 & -2i\cos(p_1) & 0 & -ie^{-iK_3}p_3 & 0 & 0 \\
		0 & -ie^{-iK_2}p_2 & ie^{iK_3}p_3 & 0 & ie^{iK_2}p_2 & -ie^{-iK_3}p_3\\
		ie^{iK_2}p_2 & 0 & 0 & -ie^{-iK_2}p_2 & 0 & -2\sin(p_1) \\
		-ie^{-iK_3}p_3 & 0 & 0 & ie^{iK_3}p_3 & -2\cos(p_1) & 0
	\end{pmatrix} 
\end{equation}

\begin{align*}

	p_i &\equiv \vec{p}\cdot\vec{\delta}_i
\end{align*}
