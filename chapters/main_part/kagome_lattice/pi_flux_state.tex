\subsubsection{Real Space}

This system is a monoclinic (\textcolor{red}{Is this correct?}) Bravais-lattice with a six site unit cell. It emerges from the bare Kagome lattice through unit cell doubling (the stranslational symmetry gets broken by the internal mean field).

\subsubsection{Symmetries}

On the level of gauge-invaiant quantuities (that is plaquette fluxes), the system does not break any lattice symmetries, that the bare Kagome lattice has. \\

The specific gauge choice however looks like it lowers the translational symmetry in one direction ($\vec{t}_2$). Obviously this is a problem of the gauge-choice, the correct symmetries are dictated by the gauge-independent observables. To "repair" this one can supplement lattice symmetry transforamtions with gauge transforamtions, as gauge transformations are always allowed. This whole idea was formally introduced here: \cite{Wen2002}. Specifically one can translate the system by $\vec{t}_1/2$ (the symmetry operation of the bare kagome lattice) followed by a gauge transformation. The correct gauge transforamtion in this case is

The characteristic polynomial of \ref{Hpirs} is (this was generate by mathematica)

\begin{equation}
	x^6-12x^4-8x^3+x^2(30 + 2\Delta_{\vec{q}})+x(24+8\Delta_{\vec{q}})+8\Delta_{\vec{q}}-8 
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{units!} with 

\begin{equation}
	\Delta_{\vec{q}} = -\cos(4q_2)+\cos(4q_3)-\cos(4q_1).
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{I should verify this uisng Cayley-Hamilton.} To derive analytical expressions for the bands I first divide out the two flat bands (i.e. polynomial division by $(x+2)^2$). The result is

\begin{equation}
	x^4-4x^3+8x-2+2\Delta_{\vec{q}}.
\end{equation}

To make progress I postulate (judging from the numeriacl plot of the dispersion) that the above polynomial can be written as a product of two quadratic polynomials. Furtheremore the solutions of said qaudratic equations must be centered around $t$. The p-q forumla yields that the linear coefficents of both quadratic polynomials must therefore be $-2t$.

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\Rightarrow x^4-4x^3+8x-2+2\Delta_{\vec{q}} &=(x^2-2x+a)\cdot(x^2-2x +b) \\
		&= x^4-4x^3+x^2(4+a+b)-2x(a+b)+ab.
	\end{split}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
	\Rightarrow a+b \overset{!}{=} -4, \quad ab \overset{!}{=} -2+2\Delta_{\vec{q}}.
\end{equation}

These equations are solved by 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		a_{1,2} &= -2 \pm \sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}} \\
		b_{1,2} &= -2 \mp \sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}.
	\end{split}
\end{align}

Both solution lead to the same result ($a$ and $b$ are exchanged in the second soltuion wrt the first one). This gives the following expressions for the bands 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\varepsilon_1 (\vec{q}) &= -2t \\
		\varepsilon_2 (\vec{q}) &= -2t \\
		\varepsilon_3 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 -\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t \\
		\varepsilon_4 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 -\sqrt{3-\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t \\
		\varepsilon_5 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 +\sqrt{3-\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t \\
		\varepsilon_6 (\vec{q}) &= \left(1 +\sqrt{3+\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}}\right)t.
	\end{split}
\end{align}

\begin{equation}
	\Delta_{\vec{q}} = -\cos(4q_2)+\cos(4q_3)-\cos(4q_1).
\end{equation}

At half filling the lowest three bands are filled, thus the Fermi-energy is between the yellow and green band.

\begin{center}
	\includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{touching_points_4th_band.png}
\end{center}

Shown above is the 4th band (green band). Apparently there are two flavours of touching points with one of each in the first Brillouin zone. They are time revearsel partners. From the numerical data these tow touching points lie at

\begin{equation}
	\vec{K} = \frac{\pi}{4}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		1 \\
		1/\sqrt{3}
	\end{pmatrix}, \quad
	\vec{K}^\prime = -\frac{\pi}{4}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		1 \\
		1/\sqrt{3}
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}


Now I calculate the various touching points. It is clear from the expressions above that bands 3 and 4 as well as bands 5 and 6 touch iff 

\begin{gather}
	6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}} = 0 \notag \\
	\Leftrightarrow -\cos(4q_2)+\cos(4q_3)-\cos(4q_1) = 3 \notag \\
	\Leftrightarrow 2\sin(2q_x)\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) - \cos(4q_x) = 3.
\end{gather}

This can easily be solved considering the ranges of sine and cosine. A possible parametrization of the solution is 

\begin{align}
	q_x &= (-1)^s \frac{\pi}{4} + m_1 \pi \\
	q_y &= (-1)^s \frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{3}} +(m_1 + m_2)\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}
\end{align}

On the other hand, bands 4 and 5 touch when 

\begin{gather}
	\sqrt{3-\sqrt{6-2\Delta_{\vec{q}}}} = 0 \notag \\
	\Leftrightarrow 2\Delta_{\vec{q}} = -3 \\
	\Leftrightarrow 4\sin(2q_x)\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) - 2\cos(4q_x) = -3 \notag
\end{gather}

Using $\cos(2x) = 1-2\sin^2(x)$ I obtain

\begin{gather}
	\sin^2(2q_x) + \sin(2q_x)\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) + \frac14 = 0 \notag \\
	\Rightarrow \sin(2q_x) = \frac12\left( -\sin(2\sqrt{3}q_y) \pm \sqrt{\sin^2(2\sqrt{3}q_y) - 1}\right)
\end{gather}

The expression under the square root gives a condition for $q_y$. It tourns out there are two distinct sets of solutions (meaning there are two distinct sets of equivalent touching points).

\begin{align}
	q_x &= (-1)^s \frac{\pi}{12} + m_1  \pi \\
	q_y &= (-1)^{s+1} \frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{3}} + (m_1+m_2)\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}
\end{align}

and 

\begin{align}
	q_x &= (-1)^s\frac{5\pi}{12} + m_1 \pi \\
	q_y &= (-1)^s\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{4} + (m_1 + m_2)\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}
\end{align}

The relevant equations are 

\begin{equation}
	t(\vec{\delta}_{ij})e^{i\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}}  \approx t e^{i\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}}\left(1+i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij} - \beta \left( \vec{\delta}_{ij} \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij}\right) + i \left(\vec{K} \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

and 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		t(\vec{\delta}_{ij})e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}} + t(-\vec{\delta}_{ij})e^{-i\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}}  \approx & 2t\cos(	\vec{q}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}) - 2t\sin(\vec{q} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij})\left(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}\right) \\
		-&2\beta t\cos(\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij})\left(\vec{\delta}_{ij} \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij} \right) \\
		-&2t\sin(\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij})\left(\vec{K} \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij}\right)
	\end{split}
\end{align}

and 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		t(\vec{\delta}_{ij})e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}}-t(-\vec{\delta}_{ij})e^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}} \approx & 2it \sin(\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}) +2it\cos(\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij})\left(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij}\right) \\
		-&2i\beta t \sin(\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij})\left(\vec{\delta}_{ij} \cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij}\right) \\
		+&2it\cos(\vec{K}\cdot\vec{\delta}_{ij})\left(\vec{K}\cdot \underline{u} \cdot \vec{\delta}_{ij}\right)
	\end{split}
\end{align}

I split the everything into two parts:

\begin{gather}
	\begin{split}
		\langle \delta H_1 \rangle_{\vec{w}_1, \vec{w}_2} = -\frac{\beta}{2}(1-\sqrt{3})(u_{xx}+u_{yy})\sigma_0 \\
		\langle \delta H_2 \rangle_{\vec{w}_1, \vec{w}_2} = \sqrt{2}\frac{\pi}{4}\left(u_{xx} + \frac{u_{yx}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sigma_x + \sqrt{2}\frac{\pi}{4}\left(u_{xy} + \frac{u_{yy}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sigma_y
	\end{split}
\end{gather}

The second term is a gradient field and can be gauged away. This means that to this order there is no effective vector potential but only an effective scalar potential. \\

Doing the same calculation for $K^\prime$ basically means transforming the Hamiltonian 

\begin{equation}
	H \mapsto \tilde{H} \equiv THT,
\end{equation}

with $T$ the time revearsel. Thus, the eigenvectors of $\tilde{H}$ are $T\vec{v}_1$ and $T\vec{v}_2$ (since $T$ is its own inveres). In this case time revearsel (which can be interpreted as either $\vec{K} \mapsto -\vec{K}$ or $\vec{q} \mapsto -\vec{q}$) is equivalent to complex conjugation. Due to the antilinearity of time revearsel, the matrix elements are conjugated. Thus the parameters have to be adjusted.

\subsubsection{crossing 5-6}

Since bands 5 and 6 cross at the same point as bands 3 and 4, the expansions of the Hamiltonian are still valid. The eigenvectors corresponding to bands 5 and 6 at the $K$-point are 

\begin{align}
	\vec{v}_5(\vec{K}) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sqrt{6}+3\sqrt{2}}}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} -i\frac{2+\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}} & -2-\sqrt{3} & i\sqrt{2}(1+\sqrt{3}) & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + i \frac{2+\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{2}} & 1 & 0
	\end{pmatrix} \\
	\vec{v}_6(\vec{K}) &= \frac{1}{4\sqrt{3}}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		1+\sqrt{3} + i(1-\sqrt{3}) & \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{6} & 0 & 1+\sqrt{3} -i (1-\sqrt{3}) & \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{6} & 4
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}

The projections otno $\vec{v}_5(\vec{K})$ and $\vec{v}_6(\vec{K})$ are

\begin{gather}
	\begin{split}
		\vec{v}_5^H \cdot H_{\vec{K}} \cdot \vec{v}_5 &= (1+\sqrt{3})t + \frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_x - \frac{1-\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_y \\
		\vec{v}_6^H \cdot H_{\vec{K}} \cdot \vec{v}_6 &= (1+\sqrt{3})t-\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_x + \frac{1-\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_y \\
		\vec{v}_5^H \cdot H_{\vec{K}} \cdot \vec{v}_6 &= \frac{1-\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_x + \frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_y \\
		\vec{v}_6^H \cdot H_{\vec{K}} \cdot \vec{v}_5 &= \frac{1-\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_x + \frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}t p_y
	\end{split}
\end{gather}

\begin{equation}
	\Rightarrow \langle H_{\vec{K}} \rangle_{\vec{v}_5, \vec{v}_6} = t(1+\sqrt{3})\sigma_0 + \frac{t}{2}\left( (1+\sqrt{3})p_x - (1-\sqrt{3})p_y\right)\sigma_z + \frac{t}{2}\left((1-\sqrt{3})p_x + (1+\sqrt{3})p_y\right) \sigma_x
\end{equation}

Thus, the dispersions are

\begin{align}
	\varepsilon_{5\vec{K}}(\vec{p}) &= (1+\sqrt{3})t - \sqrt{2}t\sqrt{p_x^2 + p_y^2} \\
	\varepsilon_{6\vec{K}}(\vec{p}) &= (1+\sqrt{3})t + \sqrt{2}t\sqrt{p_x^2 + p_y^2}
\end{align}

Again the Fermi velocity is $v_F= \sqrt{2}t$. I use the usual method and refine it a bit. Consider the coefficients 

\begin{align}
	&H_{11} \cos(\phi) + H_{12} \sin(\phi) \cos(\kappa) & (\sigma_z) \label{coeffz}\\
	&H_{11} \sin(\phi)\cos(\theta) + H_{12}\left( \sin(\kappa) \sin(\theta) -\cos(\phi)\cos(\kappa)\cos(\theta)\right) &(\sigma_x) \label{coeffx} \\
	&H_{11} \sin(\phi) \sin(\theta) - H_{12} \left( \sin(\kappa) \cos(\theta) + \cos(\phi) \cos(\kappa) \sin(\theta)\right) &(\sigma_y) \label{coeffy}
\end{align}

I used the shorthand $H_{ii} \equiv \vec{v}_i^T \cdot H_{\vec{K}} \cdot \vec{v}_i$. Going forward I split these coefficients into $p_x$ and $p_y$ parts, which I should call $H_{ii}^x$ and $H_{ii}^y$, such that $H_{ii} = H_{ii}^x p_x + H_{ii}^y p_y$. \\

Solving for an equation of the form $H = \varepsilon_0 \sigma_o + p_x \sigma_x + p_y \sigma_y$, eq \ref{coeffz} directly gives

\begin{gather*}
	\cos(\kappa) = 0 \\
	\cos(\phi) = 0,
\end{gather*}

assuming that $H_{11}^{x/y} \not= 0$ and $H_{12}^{x/y} \not= 0$. \textcolor{red}{This is not enough to fix $\kappa$ and $\phi$ yet as both $\pi/2$ and $-\pi/2$ are valid solutions. (I lack a rigorous arguent here)}. Equations \ref{coeffx} and \ref{coeffy} reveal

\begin{equation}
	\tan(\theta) = - \frac{H_{12}^x}{H_{12}^y}.
\end{equation}

In this case that means 

\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\theta &= \arctan\left(-\frac{1-\sqrt{3}}{1+\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{\pi}{12}\\
		\phi &= \pi/2 \\
		\kappa &= - \pi/2
	\end{split}
\end{align}

This resulting strain is 

\begin{align}
	\langle \delta H_1 \rangle_{\vec{w}_5, \vec{w}_6} &= -\frac{\beta}{2}(1+\sqrt{3})(u_{xx} + u_{yy})\sigma_0 \\
	\langle \delta H_2 \rangle_{\vec{w}_5, \vec{w}_6} &= \sqrt{2}\frac{\pi}{4}\left(u_{xx} + \frac{u_{yx}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)\sigma_x + \sqrt{2}\frac{\pi}{4} \left(u_{xy} + \frac{u_{yy}}{\sqrt{3}}\right)  \sigma_y
\end{align}
